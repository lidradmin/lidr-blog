Site photo timestamps turn a picture into evidence. A photo of a rebar cage shows what was built. A timestamped photo shows what was built, and when, before the concrete went in and covered it for good. Without the date and time, a site photo is a nice picture. With them, it is a record.

This post explains why every site photo needs a timestamp, what goes wrong without one, and how to make timestamps part of the routine rather than an afterthought.

## Why site photo timestamps matter

Most of what gets photographed on site is about to disappear. Reinforcement gets cast into slabs. Pipework gets boxed in. Insulation gets boarded over. Once that happens, the photo is the only proof of what was there, and the question people ask about it is almost always "when?"

A timestamp answers that question without anyone having to remember.

### Proving work was done before it was covered

If a client, inspector or engineer later asks whether the cover to reinforcement was checked before the pour, a photo of the cage helps. A photo dated the morning of the pour helps far more. It ties the image to a specific point in the build sequence, so nobody has to argue about whether it was taken before or after.

### Settling disputes about delays and variations

Disagreements about programme slip or extra work usually come down to dates. Who was waiting on whom? When was the area handed over? When did the instruction arrive? Photos with reliable timestamps let you show the state of an area on a given day rather than relying on memory or a diary entry written later.

### Making progress reports believable

A progress report full of undated photos invites the question "is this current?" Dated photos let the reader see at a glance that the images reflect this week, not last month. That makes the report quicker to read and easier to trust.

### Tracking snags from raised to closed

A snag photo is most useful as a pair: one when the defect is raised, one when it is fixed. Timestamps show how long it stayed open and prove it was closed out. ## What goes wrong without timestamps

Most phones do record the time a photo was taken, hidden in the file's metadata. The trouble is how easily that information gets lost or muddled:

- **Messaging apps and email** often strip metadata when photos are shared or compressed.
- **Copying files between devices** can replace the original capture time with the date the file was copied.
- **Screenshots and re-saved images** carry the date they were made, not the date of the original photo.
- **Folders named by hand** ("Level 2 pour") tell you the area but not the day, and photos end up in the wrong folder.
- **Shared drives with hundreds of images** become impossible to sort into the order things actually happened.

The result is a folder of photos nobody can put in sequence with confidence, which is exactly when you need them most.

## How to make timestamps part of the routine

You do not need a complicated process. You need a few habits that hold up when the site is busy.

### Capture the time when the photo is taken

The most reliable timestamp is the one recorded at the moment of capture, by the tool you take the photo with. Relying on someone to type a date into a file name later is where mistakes creep in.

### Keep the timestamp visible, not just hidden

Metadata is useful, but a date and time shown on or alongside the photo is what a reader actually sees in a report. It saves them opening file properties to check.

### Add location and a short note

A timestamp says when. A location tag says where. A one-line note says what and why: "Column C4 cage, ready for inspection". Together they make a photo that someone who was not on site can understand.

### Share photos without losing the record

Avoid sending evidence photos through channels that strip or overwrite the original details. Keep the originals in one place and share reports or links from there.

## A quick checklist for every evidence photo

Before you put the phone away, check that each photo has:

- [ ] The date and time it was taken
- [ ] Where on site it was taken
- [ ] A short note saying what it shows
- [ ] A clear view of the work, with some context around it
- [ ] Been saved somewhere the team can find it, not just your camera roll

## The short version

Site photos are often the only record of work that is about to be hidden. A timestamp is what turns that record into evidence. Capture it at the moment the photo is taken, keep it visible, add a location and a short note, and keep the originals somewhere safe. When a question comes up months later, you will be glad you did.
