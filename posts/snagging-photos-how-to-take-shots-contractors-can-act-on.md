Good snagging photos answer three questions without anyone needing to ring you: where is the defect, what exactly is wrong, and what does "fixed" look like? The simplest way to get there is to take three shots of every snag – a wide shot for location, a mid shot for the element, and a close-up for the defect – then number, mark up and describe it before you move on.

This guide is for site managers and small builders doing a handover walkthrough. It covers planning the walk, taking each shot, labelling the snag so the right trade can fix it, and checking the work when the contractor comes back.

## Why most snagging photos don't help

A close-up of a scratch on a door could be any door in the building. A photo of a whole room with a tiny chip in the skirting is just a photo of a room. Both come back with "which one?", and someone wastes a trip.

The usual problems are:

- Too close, so there's no way to tell where it is
- Too far away, so the defect can't be seen
- No description, or a vague one like "poor finish"
- Photos from different plots or flats mixed together
- No record of what was agreed as fixed

All of these are easy to avoid if you follow the same routine for every snag.

## Plan the walkthrough before you start

### Use the same names as the drawings

Name every plot, flat and room the same way each time, for example "Plot 4 – Bedroom 2". Use the names on the drawings and room schedule, so the contractor can match your photos to their paperwork without guessing.

### Walk in a fixed order

Pick a route and stick to it. Start at the front door, then work through each room clockwise, ceiling to floor. A fixed route makes your photos easy to follow in order, and it makes a missed room obvious.

### Take the right kit

- Phone, charged
- A torch
- A tape measure or a pen for scale
- Low-tack sticky notes for snag numbers
- The drawings or room schedule

## The three-shot method for each snag

### 1. Wide shot: where is it?

Stand in the doorway or a corner. Get enough of the room in frame to recognise it: the window, the door, the radiator. Someone who has never been in the building should be able to walk straight to the spot.

### 2. Mid shot: what is it on?

Fill the frame with the element: the door, the section of wall, the sink. This shows which side, which panel or which tap, which a close-up alone can't.

### 3. Close-up: what's wrong?

Get close enough that the defect is obvious at a glance. Lay a tape measure or pen next to it for scale, so a hairline crack doesn't look like a gap, or the other way round.

### Get the light right

- Shine a torch flat along walls and ceilings. Raking light shows plaster and paint defects that disappear under flat light.
- Turn the flash off on glossy surfaces like tiles, glass and gloss paint. Glare hides the defect. Change your angle instead.
- Tap the screen on the defect so the camera focuses on it, not the background.
- Check each close-up before you move on. A blurred close-up is the most common reason for a second visit.

## Label, mark up and describe every snag

### Number it

Give each snag a number that matches your snag list. If you put a sticky note with the number in the wide shot, use a low-tack one and take it off afterwards.

### Mark it up

Add an arrow or a circle to point at the defect. Don't draw over the defect itself, as the contractor needs to see it clearly. Keep any text on the photo short.

### Write a description a stranger could act on

Use the same pattern every time: **location – element – defect – what you expect**.

For example: "Plot 4, Bedroom 2, door to landing: paint scuffed through on hinge-side edge. Fill, sand and repaint to match."

Compare vague and specific descriptions:

- "Poor finish" becomes "Living room ceiling above window: roller marks visible in side light. Re-coat ceiling."
- "Leak" becomes "Kitchen sink: waste dripping at trap joint when tap runs. Reseat or replace trap."
- "Mastic" becomes "Bathroom, bath at tap end: gap in silicone at wall corner. Remove and reapply."

### Say which trade it's for

Tag each snag with the trade: joiner, painter, plumber, electrician. That way the list can be split up and each trade only sees its own items.

## Share the list so it actually gets fixed

The contractor needs one document organised by location. It should list every numbered snag with its three photos and description, in the same order you walked the building. Send it before they come back, not on the day.

Agree up front how they'll confirm each item is done, so everyone knows what "closed" means.

## Check the fix with a matching photo

When you go back to check, take a new close-up from the same spot with the same framing as the original. Put it next to the first close-up and the before and after makes it clear what was done.

- If it's fixed, mark the snag closed.
- If it isn't, take a fresh photo, add a note saying why, and leave it open.

Don't delete the original photos. They're your record of what was found and when.

## Snagging photos checklist

Before you leave each room:

- Wide, mid and close-up taken for every snag
- Scale reference in each close-up
- Close-ups checked for focus and glare
- Snag numbered and matched to the list
- Arrow or circle added, defect not covered
- Description follows location – element – defect – expected result
- Trade tagged

Before you send the list:

- Snags in walking order, grouped by plot and room
- Every snag has all three photos
- Agreed how fixes will be confirmed

When the contractor has been back:

- Matching close-up taken from the same spot
- Each snag marked closed or left open with a reason
- Original photos kept
